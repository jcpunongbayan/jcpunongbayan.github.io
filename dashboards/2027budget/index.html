National expenditure program

Proposed 2027 budget
The real score

JC Punongbayan

Every August the President sends Congress a budget proposal, called the National Expenditure Program (NEP). Every December Congress sends back a law, the General Appropriations Act (GAA). What changes in between is where our budget is actually decided. This page measures it, using nine documents covering 2023 to 2027.

01 · Composition The 2027 proposal

Two fifths of the budget is spoken for before anyone deliberates

Automatic appropriationProgrammed

The 13 biggest items in the 2027 proposal, out of 103. Each ordinary department is shown as a single total. The large automatic and lump-sum funds are broken into their parts.

Local governments' share of national taxes and interest on the national debt come to P2,434 billion between them.

That is 33.8% of the entire proposal.

Nobody sits down each year and decides those two amounts. One is a share of taxes collected three years earlier, fixed by law. The other is whatever we owe our lenders.

Add the BARMM block grant (the yearly transfer to the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao), pensions, and the retirement premiums of government workers. About 40% of the budget is spoken for before anyone chooses anything.

But the fight is over what remains. Education and public works alone take 22.5% of the total.

02 · Size Budget against the economy, 2023–2027

The budget barely moves 
as a share of the economy

Measured economyForecast economy

Total appropriations as a % of nominal gross domestic product. GDP for 2023 to 2025 is the actual outturn from the Philippine Statistics Authority. GDP for 2026 and 2027 is the low end of the range the government projected alongside the 2027 proposal, which is the basis it used for its own published 21.7% figure.

A budget of P7.2 trillion sounds enormous. Set against the size of the economy, it is almost exactly what it was four years ago.

The budget has sat between 21.7% and 22.6% of gross domestic product (GDP), the value of everything the country produces in a year, in every year since 2023.

The peak is 2025, at 22.6%. That was not a spending surge. The budget grew 9.7% that year while the economy grew 5.9% in peso terms, so the same money became a larger slice of a smaller economy than anyone had planned for.

But the last two bars rest on a forecast. The economy of 2026 and 2027 has not happened yet, so those two ratios use the government's own projections rather than a measurement.

Note that those projections have already missed once. For 2025 the government projected nominal GDP of P28,356 billion at the low end of its range. The outturn was P28,014 billion, short by P342 billion.

I flag this because the advertised 21.7% for 2027 assumes the economy grows 8.8% in peso terms. Miss by as much as 2025 missed, and the ratio is 22.0% instead.

Which budget

This counts appropriations, meaning the spending authority written into the budget documents. The government's headline figures usually count disbursements, the cash that actually goes out, which is lower. For 2026 it reports disbursements at 21.2% of GDP against the 22.3% shown here. Both are right. They are not the same measure.

03 · Measurement Proposal vs law, 2023–2026

Congress tends to modify the budget
at the level of programs, not agencies

How much of the budget Congress moves. Read each bar this way: that share of every peso would have to change hands to turn the President's proposal into the law that was signed. The same question, asked at four levels of detail.

Look only at department totals and Congress seems to move about 6% of the budget. Look at individual programs and it moves 16–23%.

But that gap is the point. Roughly three quarters of the money Congress moves stays inside the same department, taken from one of its programs and given to another.

Judge the budget by department totals and all of that is invisible.

Note that going finer still, down to individual funds and expense types, barely changes the answer.

The real bargaining happens at the level of programs.

Worth knowing

Congress cannot make the budget bigger. The Constitution caps it at the President's total. So everything here is money moved from one purpose to another, never money added.

04 · Persistence Four years, four handovers

The President tends to recycle
past budget proposals

Each dot is one year's survival rate, which I call lambda (λ). The grey line through it is the margin of error. A value of 1.0 would mean the President's next proposal fully accepts what Congress enacted. A value of 0 would mean he ignores it completely and starts again from his own earlier figure.

Congress passes the budget. The President then writes next year's proposal. The question here is simple: how much of what Congress changed does he keep?

The answer, every year, is between 30% and 50%. Raise a program by P10 billion and the next proposal typically keeps only P3 to P5 billion of that increase. The rest is quietly pulled back toward what the President asked for in the first place.

That survival rate is what lambda measures. Note that it is a share, not a peso amount, which is why it can be compared across four very different years.

But the mirror image matters just as much. The President's own previous request carries roughly twice the weight of the law that was actually signed.

Confidential funds at the Department of the Interior and Local Government (DILG) show it plainly. They were proposed at exactly P0.907 billion four years running, raised by Congress three times out of four, and proposed again unchanged each time.

What lambda misses

This tracks whether the pesos survive, not whether the projects do. A program can keep the same funding level from one year to the next while the specific roads, schools, or beneficiaries inside it change entirely.

05 · Reversion 2026 proposal → 2026 law → 2027 proposal

What Congress added in 2026, the 2027 proposal takes back

Congress changed itThe 2027 proposal changed it back

Change in funding, P billion. Where the two bars point in opposite directions, the President's next proposal is undoing what Congress did. Where they point the same way, it is going further. AICS is the Assistance to Individuals in Crisis Situations, AKAP the Ayuda sa Kapos ang Kita Program, TUPAD the labor department's emergency employment program, PAFF the Presidential Assistance to Farmers and Fisherfolk, and 4Ps the Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program.

Medical assistance goes P24.24 billion, then P51.65 billion, then P24.24 billion. Back to the centavo.

The same shape repeats across the assistance programs. Of the P500 billion Congress added across government, P283 billion is taken out again in the next proposal. Of what it cut, P306 billion is put back.

But the local government support fund is the exception. Congress raised it by P41.8 billion and the President kept the higher figure. It is the only large fund on this list where what Congress enacted became the new starting point.

Note that a year earlier the same fund was raised and then pulled back down.

So this is a change of behavior, not a rule.

06 · Ayuda Cash and in-kind assistance, 2023–2027

Ayuda is proposed small and enacted large, four years running

ProposedEnacted

The three biggest discretionary assistance programs added together, P billion. Blue figures show how much Congress added. AICS is the Assistance to Individuals in Crisis Situations, counted with AKAP because the two share one program code. MAIFIP is Medical Assistance to Indigent and Financially-Incapacitated Patients. TUPAD is the labor department’s emergency employment program.

In every year we can compare, Congress enacted more ayuda than the President proposed. There is no exception.

The gap is not small. The enacted figure runs between 1.5 and 2.6 times the proposal.

Congress added P31.8 billion in 2023, P92.6 billion in 2024, P53.5 billion in 2025, and P75.0 billion in 2026.

But 4Ps moves the other way. The Pantawid Pamilyang Pilipino Program (4Ps) is our largest cash transfer and the one with the tightest rules on who qualifies. In the 2025 law, Congress cut it from P114.2 billion to P64.2 billion.

Note where the missing P50 billion went. It reappears in the 2025 standby list, as purpose 23, at exactly P50,000,000,000. That money is released only if extra revenue turns up.

So in one budget, Congress raised the assistance that officials hand out case by case, and made the rules-based transfer conditional on money that may never arrive.

Two readings

These programs are also our main emergency safety net, so a rise in them fits patronage and a real expansion of help equally well. Nothing in the budget documents separates the two. I flag this because telling them apart needs beneficiary-level data, which is not published.

07 · Local funds Transfers to local governments, 2023–2027

Significant jump in the Local
Government Support Fund

ProposedEnacted

The Local Government Support Fund (LGSF) in each of the nine budget documents, P billion. Blue figures show how much Congress added. In 2023 Congress cut the fund instead, by P12.4 billion.

Four big streams carry national money to local governments. Three of them are set by formula, and Congress has never changed any of the three by a single peso.

The National Tax Allotment, the special shares of local governments in national taxes, and the BARMM block grant are identical in the proposal and in the law, in all four years.

The fourth stream has no formula. The Local Government Support Fund (LGSF) is a lump sum, and it is the one that moves.

In the 2026 budget it went from P16.1 billion proposed to P57.9 billion enacted. That is a rise of 260% in one passage through Congress.

But almost all of the increase sits in a single line inside it. Financial Assistance to Local Government Units, which carries no allocation rule at all, went from P5.0 billion to P37.5 billion. The Growth Equity Fund added another P10.3 billion.

Note that the President kept the higher figure. The LGSF is proposed at P58.5 billion for 2027, with Financial Assistance to LGUs held at exactly P37.5 billion.

The 2023 exception

Congress does not always add. In 2023 it cut the LGSF from P28.9 billion to P16.5 billion. The 2026 jump is a break in the pattern rather than the pattern itself, and I flag that one year is thin evidence for a claim about behavior.

08 · Public works Public works, 2023–2027

Flood control held flat for 
3 years, then fell 98%

ProposedEnacted

Flood control funding at the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH), in each of the nine budget documents from 2023 to 2027. P billion.

P254.3 billion, then P249.8 billion, then P250.8 billion. Then P4.8 billion.

Three budgets in a row within P4.5 billion of each other, then a cut of 98%, then a partial return to P103.5 billion in the 2027 proposal.

Whatever happened in the 2026 budget, it was not a gradual change of priorities.

But the money did not stay in the department. The DPWH fell from P881.3 billion to P530.9 billion, while its local roads, bridges, and multipurpose halls rose by only P73 billion.

Most of the missing P246 billion left the department altogether, for health, social welfare, and the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth).

The project list

The 18,172 individually named flood control projects in the 2026 law were not written in by Congress. The full list of 190,777 projects is already in the President's proposal, listed at zero. Signing the budget attaches money to entries that are there all along. Counting named projects tells you very little about who chose them.

09 · Transport Transport department, 2027 proposal

The transport budget is a 
two-project railway budget

Railway projects with money attached in the 2027 proposal, P billion. Seventeen more rail projects are named in the same document with nothing beside them. LRT is the Light Rail Transit, MRT the Metro Rail Transit, and PNR the Philippine National Railways.

The North–South Commuter Railway and the Metro Manila Subway take P191.3 billion. That is 97% of all rail money and 64% of the entire Department of Transportation (DOTr).

Everything else on rails, meaning the whole Light Rail Transit (LRT) and Metro Rail Transit (MRT) network plus the Philippine National Railways (PNR), comes to P6.3 billion between them.

Note that two thirds of the two mega-projects is borrowed money: P56.7 billion from the Asian Development Bank (ADB) and P67.7 billion from Japan.

But seventeen rail projects sit at zero, among them the Mindanao Railway, PNR South Long Haul, MRT-4, and the LRT-2 extensions.

Zero here means zero in the regular budget. Projects like these usually sit on the standby list instead, which is the next section.

10 · Standby authority The standby list, 2023–2026

Tempering of unprogrammed funds

ProposedEnacted

Unprogrammed appropriations, meaning spending Congress approves in advance but which can only be released if extra revenue, a new tax, or a loan actually comes in. It sits outside the regular budget, so none of it is counted in the other figures on this page.

Congress added P219 billion, then P450 billion, then P373 billion to the standby list in three straight years. Then, in 2026, nothing.

This is where Congress has a free hand. The regular budget has a ceiling it cannot raise. The standby list does not.

In fact, the P450 billion added in 2024 is nearly as large as everything Congress shifted around inside the regular budget that whole year.

But 2026 breaks the pattern.

For the first time, the signed figure came in P6.6 billion below what the President proposed. That is the same year Congress moved P500 billion around inside the regular budget instead.

Three readings

The timing fits a shift away from standby authority toward ordinary assistance programs. Sure, but it fits two other stories just as well. The flood control scandal may have changed what was defensible, or a weaker revenue outlook may have made standby authority pointless. I flag this because the budget documents alone cannot tell the three apart.

11 · Discretion Confidential and intelligence funds

Confidential and intelligence 
funds are still sticky

ProposedEnacted

Confidential and intelligence funds across all departments, added together, P billion. The blue figure above each pair is how much Congress added.

In no year between 2023 and 2027 did Congress cut these funds. Not once.

2024 is where the pattern shifts. It is the only budget where confidential funds end up below the proposal, from P4.86 billion down to P4.11 billion, as the Office of the Vice President, the Department of Education (DepEd), and the Department of Foreign Affairs (DFA) are all zeroed out.

But in the same document, intelligence funds rise from P5.28 billion to P8.27 billion, almost all of it in the security agencies. The money moved from civilian offices to security ones, and the total still went up.

Note that the Office of the President has sat at P4.560 billion in eight documents in a row, untouched by either branch.

That is 42% of everything proposed for 2027.

A sense of scale

These funds are about a fifth of 1% of the national budget. The attention they get is far larger than their share of the money. Sure, that can be defended on accountability grounds, since spending nobody can audit in the normal way deserves scrutiny. But it is not an argument about fiscal weight, and I think the two should not be confused.

12 · Limits

Where the numbers come from, and where they stop

Every budget figure on this page comes from the government's own documents. That means the President's proposal and the budget law as signed, for each year from 2023 to 2027, plus the separate volumes covering the standby list. The economy figures in section 02 come from the Philippine Statistics Authority for 2023 to 2025 and from the government's own projections for 2026 and 2027.

Three limits worth stating

  • "Congress" is a shorthand. The difference between proposal and law bundles together House amendments, Senate amendments, the closed-door reconciliation between the two chambers, technical adjustments by the Department of Budget and Management (DBM), and items the President struck out before signing. This page cannot say which of those did what.
  • Programs get renamed and reshuffled. Comparing any single program across years means trusting that the thing called X this year is the thing called X last year. Usually it is. But sometimes a program is split, merged, or renamed, and I flag that any year-to-year comparison of it should be read with that in mind.
  • A budget is permission to spend, not spending. Nothing here shows what was actually released, awarded, or built. Much of the discretion we worry about happens after the budget is signed, in when money is released and to whom contracts go. None of that is visible in these documents.

The words used here

  • Proposal (NEP). The National Expenditure Program, or what the President asks Congress for, submitted every August.
  • The law (GAA). The General Appropriations Act, or what Congress passes and the President signs, usually in December.
  • Regular budget. Spending funded by revenue the government expects to have. Its total is capped at what the President proposed.
  • Standby list. Spending approved in advance but released only if extra money turns up. No cap.
  • Lambda (λ). The share of a change made by Congress that survives into the President's next proposal.
